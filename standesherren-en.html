Standesherren: Concept and History

The term “Standesherr” repeatedly causes confusion and is not immediately clear to the layperson. The Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation distinguished between high and lower nobility. Those who held a seat and a vote in the Imperial Diet counted as high nobility. The Diet met permanently in Regensburg from 1663 and was divided into three colleges: the Electoral College, the Council of Princes and the Council of Imperial Cities.

The Council of Princes included not only princes but also counts and barons. Around 1800 it comprised 100 votes, 37 of them ecclesiastical and 63 secular. The secular votes were divided into 59 individual votes (Virilstimmen) of the imperial princes and four collective votes (Kuriatstimmen) of the colleges of imperial counts, the so-called Counts’ Benches. A total of 124 counts and barons sat on these benches. A collective vote meant that all members of a bench together held only one vote.

In principle, membership of the imperial estates was tied to matriculated land held immediately from the Empire and owned by the family. Those who met this condition were called Realists and ruled their territory, however large or small it was. There were also imperial estates without a territory of their own, the so-called Personalists. They were provisionally admitted to the college of princes or counts on condition that they acquire an immediate imperial territory within a reasonable time. Their membership rested on personal admission and was at first often not hereditary, so that it could lapse again on the death of the holder. Moving between high and lower nobility was also not uncommon over the centuries, for example through the acquisition or sale of immediate imperial lordships.

At the end of the Old Empire there were 266 territories with imperial membership and full territorial sovereignty, namely 72 ecclesiastical and 143 secular lordships as well as 51 Free Imperial Cities. The Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803 abolished 112 imperial estates on the right bank of the Rhine alone, and the founding of the Confederation of the Rhine claimed a further 70 or so formerly independent lordships. With the abdication of the imperial crown on 6 August 1806 the Old Empire came to an end. Most imperial estates lost their independence and were mediatised, that is, placed under the territorial sovereignty of another, now sovereign state. The distinction between high and lower nobility initially lapsed. The Congress of Vienna also brought numerous further mediatisations.

Standesherren, or the mediatised, are therefore those princes and counts who lost imperial immediacy and territorial sovereignty between 1803 and 1815. In this sense the Standesherren are a result of German particularism (Kleinstaaterei). The term itself first appears in Article XIV of the German Federal Act of 8 June 1815, which confirmed the mediatised in their position within the high nobility and in their equality of birth (Ebenbürtigkeit) with the ruling houses. In 1825 the federal states reported their princely, and in 1829 their comital, standesherrlich houses to the German Federal Assembly, which settled legally who was a Standesherr within the meaning of the Federal Act. As a visible sign of equality of birth, the heads of the princely houses bore the style “Durchlaucht” (Serene Highness), those of the comital houses the style “Erlaucht” (Illustrious Highness).

A fixed cut-off date applied for recognition as a Standesherr, namely 6 August 1806, the day the imperial crown was laid down. Only those who actually held an individual or collective vote on that day were recognised as mediatised. Only the legal position on that day was decisive. If a family sold its formerly immediate possessions only after 1806, as for example the Auersperg, Colloredo, Lobkowicz, Metternich, Stadion and Trauttmansdorff-Weinsberg did, their status as Standesherren remained unaffected. Conversely, the acquisition of immediate imperial lordships after that date could no longer establish a Standesherrschaft.

The legal turning point came after the First World War. With the Weimar Constitution of 14 August 1919 the nobility was abolished as a class, and its titles counted from then on only as part of the name. Since then there has been no nobility in the legal sense.

As a body representing the interests of the mediatised, the “Verein der Standesherren” was founded in April 1864; its first president was Carl Egon, Prince of Fürstenberg. The association of the German Standesherren still exists today. It is financed exclusively by the heads of the standesherrlich houses and is devoted to preserving tradition, family archives and questions of noble law. Once a year a Standesherrentag (day of the Standesherren) is held, with a closing ball.

The standesherrlich families active today include Arenberg, Auersperg-Trautson, Bentheim und Steinfurt, Bentheim-Tecklenburg, Castell-Castell, Castell-Rüdenhausen, Colloredo-Mannsfeld, Croy, Erbach-Erbach, Erbach-Fürstenau, Esterhazy von Galantha, Fugger von Babenhausen, Fugger von Glött, Fürstenberg, Harrach, Hohenlohe-Bartenstein, Hohenlohe-Langenburg, Hohenlohe-Oehringen, Hohenlohe-Waldenburg, Isenburg, Khevenhüller-Metsch, Königsegg-Aulendorf, Kuefstein, Leiningen, Lobkowicz, Löwenstein-Wertheim-Freudenberg, Löwenstein-Wertheim-Rosenberg, Neipperg, Oettingen-Oettingen, Oettingen-Wallerstein, Orsini und Rosenberg, Ortenburg, Pappenheim, Platen-Hallermund, Quadt zu Wykradt und Isny, Ratibor, Rechberg und Rothenlöwen, Rechteren-Limpurg, Salm-Horstmar, Salm-Salm, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Berleburg, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Hohenstein, Sayn-Wittgenstein-Sayn, Schaesberg, Schönborn-Buchheim, Schönborn-Wiesentheid, Schönburg-Hartenstein, Schwarzenberg, Solms-Baruth, Solms-Hohensolms-Lich, Solms-Laubach, Solms-Rödelheim und Assenheim, Starhemberg, Stolberg-Stolberg, Stolberg-Wernigerode, Thurn und Taxis, Toerring-Jettenbach, Trauttmansdorff-Weinsberg, Waldburg zu Wolfegg, Waldburg zu Zeil, Wied, Windisch-Graetz and Ysenburg und Büdingen.

Not all houses that counted among the Standesherren in 1806 are still represented on this list. Some became extinct in the course of the 19th and 20th centuries, among them the Kaunitz-Rietberg, the Dietrichstein, the Stadion and the Metternich, to name just a few examples.
